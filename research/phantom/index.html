PHANTOM

Understanding coordinated listening.
Without guessing why it happened.

PHANTOM looks for groups of listeners whose activity moves together in unusual ways. It reconstructs the listeners and music involved, then shows the evidence for human review.

Listening events alone cannot prove why a pattern happened. A shared release, event, broadcast or fan activity can resemble manipulation.

By Nischit Girish KumarNEXUS Research InitiativeCompleted research · October 2026
The idea, in plain English

Listening together is a pattern.
It is not an explanation.

What the research found

After rejecting misleading benchmark designs, PHANTOM separated deliberately planted listening patterns from matched controls in a controlled final test. But shared stimuli can resemble organized coordination, and stress tests exposed limits in reconstructing groups. Strong structural evidence can support an investigation; it cannot, by itself, establish intent.

THE PROBLEM

A platform can see activity, but not always its cause.

Many people may play the same music at similar times. An organized campaign could cause that pattern. So could a broadcast or another shared event. Listening records alone do not automatically reveal which explanation is right.

WHAT PHANTOM DOES

Find the structure. Assemble the evidence.

PHANTOM tests for unusual coordination, reconstructs candidate groups and the tracks they focus on, and brings the evidence and its limitations together for a person to review.

WHAT IT DOES NOT DO

Declare fraud or intent from events alone.

An alert is evidence of a listening pattern. It is not a verdict about who organized it, why it happened, or whether it was fraudulent. Those questions need additional context.

WHY IT MATTERS

A high score can answer the wrong question.

A flawed test can reward shortcuts instead of real detection. Even a carefully tested detector can mistake a shared response for manipulation if its result is interpreted too broadly.

How PHANTOM works

From listening records
to a reviewable explanation.

  1. Audit the data

    Check data quality, repeated records and shortcuts that could make a test look more successful than it is.

  2. Test for coordination

    Compare timing patterns with carefully shuffled reference patterns that preserve specified listening totals.

  3. Reconstruct the structure

    Recover candidate listener groups and the tracks involved. Test this reconstruction separately from detection.

  4. Stress-test other explanations

    Challenge the result with shared events, overlapping groups and incomplete observations. Keep the failures visible.

  5. Present evidence and uncertainty

    Show a human reviewer the pattern, where it is supported, what remains unclear and which additional context could help.

This describes the research workflow. The public demo displays wholly synthetic examples; it does not run the frozen scientific detector or make decisions about real people.

The research story

Better tests changed
what the system could claim.

01 / TEST THE TEST

Reject misleading success.

Early experiments produced attractive scores for the wrong reasons: generated activity, catalog differences or duplicate records were easier to spot than coordination itself. Those results were rejected.

02 / MAKE A FAIR COMPARISON

Change the alignment, not everything else.

Matched experiments preserved each listener’s track and timestamp totals while changing how listening aligned across people. This made the intended coordination pattern the focus of the comparison.

03 / CHALLENGE THE INTERPRETATION

Keep alternative causes in view.

A shared stimulus can produce the same visible pattern as an organized campaign. Better scoring cannot recover information that is absent from the listening records.

Technical detail: benchmark failures and changes

Reject shortcuts

Generated and observed activity differed before coordination was considered. Neutral and origin probes invalidated attractive early scores.

Preserve the comparison

Matched worlds hold declared actor marginals fixed: each listener’s item and timestamp totals are preserved. Collision checks caught a duplicate mismatch that would otherwise contaminate relational evidence.

Separate ranking from alerts

A raw score ranked treatments perfectly while alerting on 22 of 180 controls. Local permutation normalization (comparison with shuffled reference data from the same local setting) was evaluated to address source-specific baselines.

Keep the hard negatives

Popularity and release shocks alerted in all 12 moderate worlds per family. Shifted overlap reduced reconstruction F1 to 0.3636. A broad operating claim failed.

Change what the system reports

PHANTOM preserves evidence, missing context about its cause and equally supported alternatives. A structural alert does not become a finding of intent.

How the September holdout was kept separate

The holdout protocol was fixed before access. Later development continued without accessing the September histories, and the pre-final manuscript was frozen before access. The separate September population was then materialized and scored. Exact execution timestamps and the full verification history remain in the provenance archive and Supplementary Appendix.

The canonical scientific evaluation ran once; planned deterministic replay covered all 756 worlds. The original verifier failed while serializing its receipt. A separate post-final forensic verifier checked the saved, unchanged results without rerunning population selection, intervention construction, the detector or null generation. Local chronology is not independent institutional verification.

Source audit, a separate part of the research

704,728,520 Deezer listening events audited.

This audit examined representation and data quality. Detector and reconstruction experiments used bounded subsets or separately sampled populations. The audit count is not detector throughput or the size of a deployment evaluation.

Broader operating claims did not hold up.

Common-stimulus, popularity-shock and release-event controls can trigger structural alerts. Shifted group overlap also weakened reconstruction. These failures rule out broader operating claims from the tested evidence.

What listening events cannot tell us

Two causes.
The same visible events.

Intentional coordination

An organizer asks a group to align its listening.

A shared external stimulus

A recurring broadcast prompts the same listening.

→

Identical users, tracks and times

If the available listening records are exactly the same in both cases, a method that sees only those records has no evidence that distinguishes the causes.

PHANTOM uses these “causal twins” to apply an established limit on what observations can identify to coordinated-listening analysis. This does not mean every real case is ambiguous. Information about exposure, referrals or an organizer could change the investigation.

The event-only identifiability limit

If all permitted observations are identical, an event-only method receives no information that separates the two accounts. Randomness independent of the cause cannot add it. This is a concrete application of established observational-equivalence reasoning, not a new general theorem or a claim that every real case is ambiguous.

Technical detail: preserving ambiguity in the interface

The read-only dashboard keeps support status, constituent evidence, causal alternatives and missing provenance together. Complete ambiguity sets (groups of alternatives equally supported by the available evidence) retain equal scientific priority through filtering and export.

Go deeper

Read the evidence.
Keep its scope in view.

The contribution is an auditable account of where coordinated-listening evidence is useful and where its interpretation fails. The papers preserve the methods, results, rejected approaches and remaining limitations.

No matched empirical CopyCatch, CatchSync or FRAUDAR baseline was run. No analyst study or production deployment was evaluated. The research does not establish comparative superiority or intent from listening events alone.
